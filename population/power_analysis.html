The Power of What We're Building

A registrar for the physical world — and why the discipline is the product.

Working analysis · for the Andy call · 2026-07-20

The simplest way to say what we are building is this: we are giving physical things a stable identity, and then letting the relationships between them become computable. Everything else — the ThingSites, the extractor, the meta-configurator, the safety graph — is downstream of that one move. It sounds modest. It is not.

01The inversion: metadata, not data

Every incumbent in this space is trying to hold the data — the payload, the readings, the documents, the copies. That is expensive, it is a liability, and it does not scale, because the world produces more data every second than anyone can store or trust. We do the opposite. We hold the metadata — the identity and the relationships — and a pointer to where the data actually lives. We never simulate data; we simulate metadata only. The registry maps to nothing: it holds the pointer, not the payload.

This is not a storage optimization. It is what makes the whole thing legal, safe, and infinite. A registrar that holds identities and edges can describe a billion things without ever copying one of them. The heavy, risky, copyrighted, regulated payload stays where it belongs — with its owner — and we hold the one thing that was always missing: a durable name for it, and a verifiable account of what it is connected to.

02The spine, the graph, the redesign

There is a chain you have said many times, and it is exactly right: without the spine you can't build the graph; without the graph you can't redesign. Identity is the spine. The moment a thing has a stable, non-fabricated identity — a GTIN, a GLN, a GDTI that actually resolves to a who — its relationships stop being prose and become edges. A temperature sensor is no longer a line in a manual; it is a node that came from a supplier, runs in a protocol, lives at a location, can be substituted by another node.

Once relationships are edges, you can compute over them. You can ask which supplier a failure traces to. You can ask what a protocol would cost if you swapped one consumable for another. You can read a merger off the graph — if Thermo can model the other instruments, you can decide who you acquire. None of that is possible from data lakes. It is only possible from a graph, and the graph is only possible from the spine.

The competitor is drowning in copies of the data. We hold the one thing the data could never give them: a trustworthy name, and the edges around it.

03Why the discipline is the moat

Anyone can scrape a website. Scraping is not the product. The product is the discipline that turns a scrape into a claim you can rely on. We never fabricate a prefix, a GLN, a GTIN, or a URN. Absence of evidence is a candidate — never a guess, never an exception. A number only becomes a candidate GTIN if its prefix falls in an assigned GS1 band. A candidate only becomes truth when the one party who can answer for it ratifies it. And a ThingDaddy record is issuable under a rule that is stricter than the standard itself — never looser.

That discipline has a formal name — SHACL, the shapes that constrain an open vocabulary into something verifiable — and it is the reason the registry is believable. It is also, counterintuitively, the growth engine. A registrar that occasionally invents an identity to look complete is worthless, because no one can trust any of it. A registrar that says "candidate — unratified" exactly when it should is one you can build law, medicine, and money on top of. The corrections are the product. We proved this on real data: the first confirmed root tied a real Samsung GTIN to a verified party with nothing fabricated, and the gate correctly refused to misread a US supplement barcode as a Swedish one.

04The ThingSite: a solution packaged per place

A website replicates. One corporate site is copied across every geography; it differentiates nothing. A ThingSite is the opposite — it differentiates by location. It is bound to a GLN, and every physical place is its own ThingSite. Every Novartis lab is a different ThingSite, because the instruments, the protocols, and the people at each place are different. We do not need to simulate every GLN or pretend to know them; we use the ones a company brings with it when it claims its own map — consent at the moment of claim.

This is why building a ThingSite is not building a page. It is building a packaged solution for a place: its identity, its instruments, its protocols, its bindings, all in one resolvable unit. Multiply that by every lab, every plant, every warehouse, and you are not building a website business. You are populating a civilization of places that can finally describe themselves in a common language.

05The meta-configurator: one engine, every industry

The highest-leverage thing we built is the part that looks the most abstract. We do not author the ontologies — those are inherited from the standards bodies. We do not configure the instances — the users do that. We build the templates in the middle, the SHACL shape sets that turn any industry's vocabulary into something issuable and verifiable. One engine validated a pharmacogenomics protocol and a manufacturing work-cell without changing a line. That is not a coincidence; it is the whole thesis.

And it means the domains compose. Food science and supplement science are not lesser cousins of pharma — they are the same solution structure with a smaller shape set. A recipe is a protocol. A chef is a step-actor. A kitchen station is a work-cell. "Sear at 200°C for ninety seconds" is a value-and-unit pair our miner already extracts. We build the strict pharma case, and we relax it down to supplements and retail — which, not by accident, are the domains where the extractor yields more, because consumer brands publish the very identity that pharma hides.

06The endgame: safety, interactions, medicine

Follow the chain one step further. The long legal leaflet inside a pharma box is not text to be read — it is a document with its own identity, a GDTI, bound to the product. Its adverse reactions, its contraindications, its allergens, its interactions become first-class edges. And an interaction is, by definition, an edge between two identified substances. Once those edges exist, the graph answers a question no document ever could: what interacts with what, across every product at once.

Now map the genome and the proteome onto the protocols those products run. Connect that to the kind of personal genetic data a 23andMe holds, in the hands of the companies who make the medicine. That is not a better database. That is a different practice of medicine — one where "what is legally true inside this box" and "what is biologically true about this person" live in the same graph, keyed by identity, candidate until ratified, nothing fabricated. We are a long way from that. But the road to it is the exact road we are already on: spine, graph, redesign.

07Where this comes from — and where it is hard

This is not a startup idea that arrived last year. It is the culmination of a metadata thesis you have been building for three decades — the data-dictionary work, the semantic repositories, the EDI trading networks that already spoke in identities before the web buried them in HTML. In a real sense we are restoring semantics the web threw away, and giving them a registrar.

The honest ledger. Three things are genuinely hard, and we should say so on the call. (1) Trust requires the answerable parties. A candidate only ratifies when the company that owns the identity participates — our registry today holds a small verified set, and growing it is a go-to-market problem, not a code problem. (2) Not every site yields. Server-rendered brands hand us identity; single-page apps hide it behind JavaScript, and the honest next upgrade is a headless render, not more parsing. (3) The graph is only as good as the extractor's discipline. Every value we surface has to carry its provenance and its candidate status, or the whole trust argument collapses. That is why we build the extractor slowly and correctly, and why the store keeps score.

08The one sentence

We are building the layer that was always missing between the physical world and the internet: a disciplined registrar of identity and relationship, that holds the metadata and points to the data, that refuses to guess, and that — precisely because it refuses to guess — becomes something you can build law, commerce, and medicine on top of. The website told you a company existed. The ThingSite tells you what is true, where, and what it is connected to.
